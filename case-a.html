Case Study A: “Say Your Name Again?”

Theme: Microaggression

Format: 4-voice — spoken dialogue + inner monologue. Assign four readers or two readers doubling inner voices.

TOM — white man, 58, longtime member

SORAYA — Iranian-American woman, 37, first-time visitor

Scene

TOM (spoken, genial): I don’t think we’ve met! I’m Tom.
TOM (inner): New faces brighten coffee hour — be welcoming, Tom.
SORAYA (spoken, warm): Lovely to meet you, Tom. I’m Soraya Rezai.
SORAYA (inner): He’s about to repeat it — happens a lot.
TOM (spoken, leaning in): So‑RYE‑ah. Wow — what a gorgeous name! Could you… say it again? I just love the sound.
TOM (inner): Nailed the pronunciation. She’ll feel seen.
SORAYA (spoken, polite): Soraya.
SORAYA (inner): Performative fascination — here we go.
TOM (spoken): Beautiful. You must get compliments like that all the time.
TOM (inner): Build rapport — she’ll stay.
SORAYA (spoken): Sometimes. I was born in Maryland, though — my parents loved Persian poetry.
SORAYA (inner): Offer context, redirect.
TOM (spoken, animated): Fascinating! Our multicultural service is next month — you could share that story from the pulpit. Maybe teach us a phrase in… Farsi, right?
TOM (inner): Great idea — celebrate diversity!
SORAYA (spoken, measured): I’ll think about it. Today I’m just hoping to find community.
SORAYA (inner): Feeling spotlighted, not welcomed.
TOM (spoken): Absolutely! Let me grab a visitor card.
TOM (inner): Mission accomplished.

Reflection Questions

  1. Intention: Tom clearly wants to make Soraya feel welcome. What does his version of “welcoming” look like — and who is it really for?
  2. Tension: Soraya says, “Today I’m just hoping to find community.” How does this land after everything Tom has said? What is she pushing back against?
  3. Attention: Tom asks Soraya to repeat her name, compliments it, asks about its meaning, asks where she’s from, and invites her to teach Farsi. What pattern do you notice in where his attention goes?
  4. Retention: Tom walks away thinking he “nailed it.” What will Soraya walk away thinking? What will she tell a friend about coffee hour?
  5. Tom’s inner monologue reveals he sees himself as a skilled welcomer. How does the gap between his self-perception and Soraya’s experience illustrate the nature of microaggressions?
  6. Have you seen or experienced moments like this in your congregation? What made them hard to name in the moment?

Facilitator Guidance

Running a 20-Minute Group Discussion

Minutes 1–5: Read Together

Assign four readers — one for Tom’s spoken lines, one for Tom’s inner monologue, one for Soraya’s spoken lines, and one for Soraya’s inner monologue. (With fewer readers, two people can each double the spoken and inner voices for one character.) Read the full scenario aloud. Ask the group to notice the gap between what is said and what is thought.

Minutes 5–12: Individual Responses

Go around the table. Each person selects one reflection question and shares their response. Encourage people to name specific lines of dialogue or inner monologue, not just general impressions. The facilitator or a recorder captures key phrases on a shared surface.

Minutes 12–20: Guided Synthesis

Draw threads between responses: “Tom’s inner monologue says ‘Mission accomplished’ — but Soraya’s says ‘Feeling spotlighted, not welcomed.’ What creates that gap?” Guide the group toward understanding that microaggressions aren’t about bad intent — they’re about impact that the speaker can’t see. Close by asking: “When have you been Tom without knowing it?”

Sample Answers

Intention: Tom wants Soraya to feel seen and valued. His inner monologue shows genuine enthusiasm — “new faces brighten coffee hour.” But his version of welcome centers her difference rather than her personhood. He’s welcoming an interesting background, not a person looking for community.

Tension: Soraya’s request is simple — she wants to find community. But by that point, Tom has already asked her to repeat her name for his pleasure, complimented it as exotic, assumed her ethnic background, and invited her to perform her heritage from the pulpit. Her line is a gentle boundary: I’m not here to be your multicultural moment.

Attention: Tom’s attention is consistently on markers of difference — the sound of her name, what it means, where she’s from, what language she speaks. He never asks what brought her in, what she’s looking for, or how she’s feeling. His curiosity is about her identity, not her experience.

Retention: Tom will remember a successful welcome. Soraya will remember being made into a spectacle. She may tell a friend: “Everyone was nice, but this one guy couldn’t stop talking about my name and where I’m from. He wanted me to teach Farsi from the pulpit. I just wanted to sit in a pew.”

The inner monologue is what makes this case study powerful. Tom’s thoughts (“Nailed the pronunciation,” “Great idea — celebrate diversity!”) reveal that he experiences each microaggression as a success. That’s the core of how microaggressions work: the person delivering them often feels good about the interaction, which is why they’re so hard to interrupt.

Moments like this are hard to name because Tom is being friendly. Soraya would risk seeming ungrateful or oversensitive if she pushed back. The social cost of naming a microaggression often falls on the person experiencing it.

More Ideal Choices

What Tom Said What Tom Could Say Instead
“Could you say it again? I just love the sound.” “Soraya — nice to meet you. What brought you in today?”
“Beautiful. You must get compliments like that all the time.” (Simply move on — her name doesn’t need to be a topic of conversation)
“You could share that story from the pulpit. Maybe teach us a phrase in Farsi, right?” “We’d love to get to know you — no pressure, just come as you are.”
Asking about her ethnic background on first meeting Let Soraya share what she chooses, when she chooses — relationship first

UU Principles in Play

Inherent worth and dignity: Soraya’s worth is not located in her name’s exoticism or her potential to diversify the congregation. She deserves to be met as a whole person.

Equity: Who carries the labor of “diversity” in our spaces? When we ask newcomers to represent their backgrounds, we place the burden of our learning on the people least positioned to refuse.

Pluralism: True pluralism doesn’t spotlight difference — it creates space where difference can exist without being performed or explained.

Accountability (8th Principle): Tom’s behavior is common and well-intentioned. Accountability means building the self-awareness to recognize when our welcome is actually a form of othering — and creating congregational culture where that pattern can be named without blame.
