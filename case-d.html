Case Study D: “Presence Over Apps”

Theme: Intergenerational Care + Pronoun Tension

Format: 4-voice — spoken dialogue + inner monologue. Assign four readers or two readers doubling inner voices.

AVERY — chaplain, non-binary, 27, uses they/them; earnest problem-solver

MRS. GREEN — congregant, woman, 82, small pension, limited mobility, shrinking social circle

Scene

AVERY (spoken, bright): Mrs. Green, lovely to see you! Have you been able to make it to Sunday service?
AVERY (inner): Maybe a neighbour’s giving her lifts.
MRS. GREEN (spoken, sighs): Not anymore, dear. No licence, and Doris’s hip won’t let her drive far now.
MRS. GREEN (inner): Another friend edged out by time.
AVERY (spoken): I could match you with another driver in the congregation — ride share?
AVERY (inner): Simple fix.
MRS. GREEN (spoken, hesitant): Most folks my age have moved or passed. I hate to ask young families to detour.
MRS. GREEN (inner): My circle’s a dwindling ring.
AVERY (spoken, upbeat pivot): How about our livestream? Do you watch the service online?
AVERY (inner): Tech bridge!
MRS. GREEN (spoken): I do click in sometimes. But it’s lonely humming to a screen. I sang in the choir for thirty-two years — nothing matches voices all around you.
MRS. GREEN (inner): I miss harmony, not video.
AVERY (inner): Okay, livestream isn’t enough. What else?
AVERY (spoken): Maybe we could fund a taxi once a month? We could crowd-source the fare.
AVERY (inner): Money gets her there.
MRS. GREEN (spoken, gentle): Taxi tips pile up, love, and I’d still sit alone at coffee hour — my pew pals are gone.
MRS. GREEN (inner): Heaping bills, thin company.
AVERY (inner): She’s shutting every door.
AVERY (spoken, strained smile): I’ll keep brainstorming. There must be a way.
AVERY (inner): Why does she sound so Eeyore?
MRS. GREEN (spoken, soft): You’re a kind young girl. It helps just knowing you care. Do you know “Blue Boat Home”? It was my solo once.
MRS. GREEN (inner): Maybe music will spark connection.
AVERY (inner): Girl again — let it slide.
AVERY (spoken): I love that hymn. Beautiful imagery.
AVERY (inner): At least we found common ground. Still, no solid solution.
MRS. GREEN (inner): If only this kind child understood I need people, not plans.

Reflection Questions

  1. Intention: Avery cycles through solutions — ride share, livestream, taxi fund. What are they trying to accomplish, and why does each solution miss?
  2. Tension: Mrs. Green says, “My pew pals are gone.” What is she really telling Avery — and what would it take for Avery to truly hear it?
  3. Attention: Mrs. Green calls Avery “young girl.” Avery’s inner monologue says “Girl again — let it slide.” What are both people experiencing in that moment, and what goes unspoken?
  4. Retention: Mrs. Green’s final inner thought is: “If only this kind child understood I need people, not plans.” Avery’s is: “No solid solution.” What does this gap reveal about how each defines “help”?
  5. This scene has two forms of misrecognition running in parallel: Avery misreads Mrs. Green’s needs, and Mrs. Green misgenders Avery. How are these connected? How are they different?
  6. Have you seen or experienced moments like this in your congregation? What makes intergenerational care particularly challenging?

Facilitator Guidance

Running a 20-Minute Group Discussion

Minutes 1–5: Read Together

Assign four readers — one for Avery’s spoken lines, one for Avery’s inner monologue, one for Mrs. Green’s spoken lines, and one for Mrs. Green’s inner monologue. Read the full scenario aloud. Ask the group to notice what each character is asking for — and what each character is offering.

Minutes 5–12: Individual Responses

Go around the table. Each person selects one reflection question and shares their response. The pronoun/misgendering dimension may surface strong feelings — hold space for both younger and older perspectives. The facilitator or a recorder captures key phrases.

Minutes 12–20: Guided Synthesis

This case study has two parallel tensions: the intergenerational care gap and the pronoun misrecognition. Guide the group to sit with both without ranking them. Ask: “What would it look like for Avery and Mrs. Green to actually see each other?” Note that both characters are experiencing a form of invisibility — Mrs. Green in the congregation, Avery in their identity. Close with: “When have you let something ‘slide’ that mattered to you? What would it take to name it gently?”

Sample Answers

Intention: Avery wants to fix the problem of Mrs. Green’s absence from church. Each solution targets logistics — transportation, technology, money — but Mrs. Green’s need is relational. She doesn’t need a ride to church; she needs to feel like someone there is waiting for her. Every solution misses because it treats isolation as a logistics problem rather than a human one.

Tension: “My pew pals are gone” is a statement about grief, loneliness, and displacement. Mrs. Green is saying: even if I get there, the community I built is gone. For Avery to truly hear it, they’d have to stop problem-solving and sit in the grief — which is uncomfortable and doesn’t produce a deliverable.

Attention: When Mrs. Green says “young girl,” she’s using language that feels natural and affectionate to her. For Avery, it’s a misgendering — a small erasure of who they are. Avery chooses to “let it slide,” absorbing a cost to maintain the relationship. Both people are navigating invisible labor: Mrs. Green works to maintain connection across a generational divide; Avery works to maintain identity in a space that doesn’t quite see them.

Retention: The gap between “no solid solution” and “I need people, not plans” is the central lesson. Avery measures success by whether a problem got solved. Mrs. Green measures it by whether she felt less alone. These are fundamentally different definitions of care — and in chaplaincy, Mrs. Green’s definition should lead.

The misgendering and the needs-misreading are connected: both involve one person failing to see the other as they actually are. They’re different in power dynamics — Avery has institutional role-power as a chaplain; Mrs. Green has generational and cultural power in the “default” status of her language. Neither is malicious. Both cause real harm. Addressing them requires the same skill: gentle, honest naming.

Intergenerational care is challenging because each generation carries different assumptions about communication, technology, pace, and what “help” means. Younger caregivers may default to efficiency and resources; older congregants may need spaciousness and relationship. Neither framework is wrong — but care requires meeting people in their framework, not ours.

More Ideal Choices

What Avery Did What Avery Could Do Instead
Offered ride share, livestream, taxi fund in rapid succession “It sounds like getting there is only part of it. What would make Sunday feel like it used to?”
Kept brainstorming solutions after each was declined Paused and asked: “What do you miss most?” — then just listened
Let the misgendering slide silently “I appreciate your kindness, Mrs. Green. I go by ‘they,’ by the way — just so you know.” (gentle, no blame)
Inner thought: “Why does she sound so Eeyore?” Recognizing that grief and realism are not negativity — they are information about what the person actually needs
Left feeling there was “no solid solution” Recognized that being present, singing “Blue Boat Home” together, and promising to sit with her at coffee hour next Sunday is a solution

UU Principles in Play

Inherent worth and dignity: Both Avery and Mrs. Green deserve to be seen as they are. Avery’s identity as non-binary and Mrs. Green’s identity as a lifelong UU elder are both worthy of recognition and respect.

Justice and equity: When congregational systems — digital tools, fast-paced formats, clustered social groups — are designed for younger, tech-fluent members, elders are structurally marginalized. Equity means redesigning, not just accommodating.

Interdependence: Mrs. Green’s thirty-two years in the choir, her decades of deacon service, and her relational wisdom are congregational assets. Avery’s energy and commitment are too. Interdependence means each generation learning from the other.

Generosity: The most generous thing Avery could offer is not a resource list but their presence — sitting with Mrs. Green at coffee hour, singing a hymn together, being the person who makes Sunday worth the two-bus trip.

Accountability (8th Principle): Ageism is a form of marginalization that UU communities often overlook. So is the erasure of non-binary identity through gendered language. Both require ongoing, gentle accountability — not once, but as a practice.
