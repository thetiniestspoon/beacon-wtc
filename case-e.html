Case Study E: “Welcome to Our Family”

Themes: Name-related microaggressions, faith-based assumptions, othering through welcome

Format: 3 readers — one for Diane, one for Amira, one for narration.

DIANE — white woman, early 60s, board member and Sunday morning greeter

AMIRA — Arab-American woman, mid-30s, first-time visitor

Scene

The lobby of a Unitarian Universalist congregation, Sunday morning. Bright windows, a welcome table with nametags and brochures. A handful of congregants mingle near the coffee station. Diane wears a “GREETER” ribbon pinned to her blazer. She spots an unfamiliar face near the entrance.

DIANE (stepping forward with a warm smile): Good morning! Welcome — is this your first time visiting us?
AMIRA: It is, yes. I saw the website and thought I’d check it out.
DIANE: Wonderful! I’m Diane — I’m on the board here and I greet most Sunday mornings. And you are?
AMIRA: Amira. Amira Haddad.
DIANE: A-meer-a? Am I saying that right?
AMIRA: Close — it’s Ah-MEE-ra. Pretty straightforward.
DIANE: Ah-MEE-ra. What a beautiful name! Does it mean something?
AMIRA: It means “princess” in Arabic. My parents liked the sound of it.
DIANE: Oh, that’s lovely. So — is your family from the Middle East originally?
AMIRA: My dad’s family came from Lebanon a couple generations back. I grew up in Columbus.
DIANE: How wonderful. You know, we had a family from Jordan visit last year — lovely people. I think they celebrated Ramadan while they were here, and our minister mentioned it during the service. We really try to honor all traditions.
AMIRA: That’s nice to hear. I’m actually coming from a church background — I grew up Presbyterian.
DIANE (a beat, eyebrows lifting): Oh! Presbyterian — really?
AMIRA (a small nod): Really.
DIANE: No, I just — I think that’s great. I didn’t mean — it’s just, with your name, I assumed — well, I shouldn’t have assumed anything. I’m sorry.
AMIRA: It’s okay.
DIANE (recovering quickly): Well, you’ll fit right in! A lot of us come from Christian backgrounds. You’ll probably find the hymns familiar, and we do a lot with scripture here too — though of course we draw from everywhere. Buddhist texts, Rumi, you name it.
AMIRA: I appreciate the range. Honestly, I’ve been exploring for a while. The Presbyterian church I grew up in was lovely, but I wanted something that sits with questions more than answers.
DIANE: That’s exactly what we’re about. And honestly, with your background — straddling two cultures like that — I bet you bring such a rich perspective. Have you ever thought about sharing your story? We do a “spiritual journeys” series and people would love to hear about navigating faith between, you know, the Arab world and American Christianity.
AMIRA (pause): I appreciate the invitation. Though I’d say I’ve mostly just navigated faith as a person — I’m not sure my story is really about two worlds.
DIANE: Of course, of course. I didn’t mean to put you in a box. Here — this has all our upcoming events. Oh, and we’re doing an interfaith potluck next month. If you wanted to bring a dish from your heritage, people would be thrilled. Last year someone brought the most amazing baklava.
AMIRA (polite smile): I’ll take a look. For today, I think I’ll just find a seat and take it all in.
DIANE: Absolutely. Grab some coffee — and the service starts in ten minutes. I’m so glad you’re here, Amira. Truly. We need more diversity in this congregation.

Amira nods, takes the brochure, and moves toward the sanctuary. Diane returns to the welcome table, satisfied.

Reflection Questions

  1. Intention: What is Diane trying to accomplish in this conversation? What does she want Amira to feel?
  2. Tension: At what moments might Amira experience discomfort, even though Diane is being friendly? What causes that discomfort?
  3. Attention: Where is Diane’s attention focused throughout the exchange — on Amira as a person, or on something else? When does her focus shift?
  4. Retention: If Amira goes home and tells a friend about her first visit, what is she most likely to remember from this conversation?
  5. What assumptions does Diane make based on Amira’s name and background? How do those assumptions shape the conversation even after Amira corrects them?
  6. What might Diane do differently if she truly wanted Amira to feel welcomed as she is, rather than welcomed as a representative of diversity?

Facilitator Guidance

Running a 20-Minute Group Discussion

Minutes 1–5: Read Together

Assign three readers — one for Diane, one for Amira, and one for the narration (stage directions in italics). Read the full scenario aloud. Ask the group to listen for moments that land differently than the speaker intended.

Minutes 5–12: Individual Responses

Go around the table. Each person selects one question and shares their response. Encourage people to name specific lines of dialogue, not just general impressions. The facilitator or a recorder captures key phrases on a shared surface (flipchart, whiteboard, or shared doc).

Minutes 12–20: Guided Synthesis

Draw threads between responses: “Several of you noticed the moment Diane said ‘really?’ — what’s happening there?” Guide the group toward identifying patterns rather than judging Diane as a character. Close by asking: “Where do you see yourself in this scenario — and what would you want someone to say to you?”

Sample Answers

Intention: Diane genuinely wants Amira to feel welcome and valued. She sees herself as an ambassador for the congregation’s openness. She likely wants Amira to return.

Tension: Several moments carry an “ouch” — the repeated focus on Amira’s name and its meaning (treating it as exotic rather than simply learning it), the question about where her family is “from,” the visible surprise at her Presbyterian background (revealing an assumption that an Arab name means a non-Christian faith), the invitation to represent “two cultures” when Amira hasn’t framed herself that way, and the closing line about “needing more diversity” (which positions Amira as a category rather than a person).

Attention: Diane’s attention is largely on Amira’s identity markers — her name, her ethnicity, her assumed faith background — rather than on what Amira is actually saying about herself. When Amira says she’s looking for a place that “sits with questions,” Diane doesn’t explore that; she pivots to how Amira’s background could serve the congregation.

Retention: Amira is likely to remember feeling seen as a diversity asset rather than as a spiritual seeker. The surprise at “Presbyterian — really?” may stick longest, because it reveals how firmly Diane had already categorized her.

Diane assumes Amira’s name signals a Muslim or non-Christian faith tradition, that her heritage makes her bicultural in a way that defines her spiritual life, and that she would naturally want to represent or share that heritage publicly. Even after correction, Diane continues to frame Amira through a lens of cultural difference — the interfaith potluck, the “heritage dish,” the “two cultures.”

Diane could focus on what Amira has said she’s looking for — a community that sits with questions — and respond to that. She could learn Amira’s name without making it a topic of conversation. She could skip the biographical questions and simply invite Amira to experience the service, following up later once a relationship exists.

More Ideal Choices

What Diane Said What Diane Could Say Instead
“What a beautiful name! Does it mean something?” “Amira — got it. Great to meet you.”
“Is your family from the Middle East originally?” (Not asked on a first meeting — let Amira share what she chooses)
“Oh! Presbyterian — really?” “Oh nice — what drew you to start exploring UU spaces?”
“Straddling two cultures like that — I bet you bring such a rich perspective.” “It sounds like you’ve been on a real journey with faith. That’s something a lot of us here relate to.”
“If you wanted to bring a dish from your heritage...” “The potluck’s a great way to meet people — hope you can make it.”
“We need more diversity in this congregation.” “I’m glad you’re here. I hope you find what you’re looking for.”

UU Principles in Play

Inherent worth and dignity: Amira deserves to be met as a whole person, not filtered through assumptions about her name or ethnicity.

Pluralism: True pluralism means not pre-assigning someone’s tradition based on appearance or heritage — and not treating any tradition as the “default.”

Equity: Equity asks us to examine who carries the burden of educating, representing, or performing diversity in our spaces — and to stop placing that burden on newcomers.

Accountability (8th Principle): Diane’s missteps aren’t malicious, but accountability means examining how even warm, well-meaning behavior can reproduce patterns of othering.
