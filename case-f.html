Case Study F: “I’ll Send You Everything”

Themes: Intergenerational disconnect, presence vs. problem-solving, resource dumping as care substitution

Format: 3 readers — one for Casey, one for Margaret, one for narration.

CASEY — facilitator, late 20s, earnest and organized

MARGARET — group member, late 70s, longtime congregant

Scene

A community room at the church, just after a weeknight small group meeting. Chairs are being pushed back. Most attendees have left. Casey is stacking handouts. Margaret is slowly gathering her coat and bag. She’s lingered, as if wanting to talk.

CASEY: Margaret, I’m glad you made it tonight. I know you mentioned last time that getting here has been tough.
MARGARET: It has, dear. My neighbor Jean used to drive me, but she moved to assisted living last month. I took two buses tonight.
CASEY: Two buses — that’s a real commitment. I’m sorry about Jean. Were you two close?
MARGARET (pause): Forty years of Thursday dinners. But I didn’t want to miss the group. This is the one place I still feel like myself.
CASEY: That really means a lot — that this space matters to you like that. How are you doing with everything?
MARGARET (small laugh): Some days are heavier than others. The house is quiet. My son calls on Sundays, but he’s in Denver, so. I tell him I’m fine.
CASEY: I hear you. And I want you to know — if getting here is a barrier, we should figure that out. Have you tried any of the ride-share apps? Uber or Lyft?
MARGARET: I’ve heard of them. I don’t have one of those phones, though — just my flip phone. It makes calls and that’s enough for me.
CASEY: Oh, sure. Well — there might be some options. I think the county has a senior transit service. Do you know about Access-a-Ride?
MARGARET: I’ve heard the name. Doris from my building tried it once and waited forty minutes in the cold.
CASEY: That’s frustrating. It’s probably gotten better since then — they updated the system. I can look into it for you.
MARGARET (pause): That’s kind. Honestly though, the ride is only part of it. I used to come early and sit with people. Now I arrive late and half the faces are new. The young folks are lovely, but — you all move so fast.
CASEY: What do you mean by that?
MARGARET: Well, like tonight — the breakout groups. Everyone was typing notes on their laptops, and I’m sitting there with my pen. Someone shared a link in the chat and I didn’t even know there was a chat.
CASEY (nodding): Yeah, I can see how that would feel isolating. We should probably do a better job of offering both options — digital and paper. I’ll flag that for next time.
MARGARET: It’s not just the technology. It’s — people pair up with the ones they already know. The younger members have their clusters. I don’t begrudge it, but I sit down and nobody quite knows what to do with me.
CASEY: That’s not okay. You belong here as much as anyone.
MARGARET (a gentle smile): I know I do. I’ve been UU longer than most people in that room have been alive. I just wonder sometimes if the church has moved on from people like me.
CASEY: It hasn’t. And it shouldn’t. Look — I want to help with this. Let me put together some things for you. There’s actually a whole list of resources — the senior transit program, the church’s ride board, a couple of volunteer driver networks. And I think there’s a buddy system we piloted last year for pairing newer members with longtime folks.
MARGARET: That buddy idea sounds nice. When I was a deacon, we just called it “checking in on each other.”
CASEY (smiling): Same spirit, different branding. But seriously — I’ll compile everything and send it your way. What’s your email?
MARGARET: I have one, but I only check it when my son tells me to look for photos.
CASEY: That’s fine — I’ll send it there and you can look when you get a chance. I’ll include the transit links, the ride board sign-up, the buddy program contact, and a couple of other things I’ve been meaning to share. Oh, and there’s a really good article about intergenerational ministry I read last week — I’ll attach that too.
MARGARET (quietly): That’s a lot of reading.
CASEY: It’s all in one email — easy to find. And if you have questions about any of it, just call me. My number’s on the church website.

Margaret nods, pulls on her coat.

MARGARET: Casey — you’re a good soul. Thank you for listening tonight.
CASEY: Of course. I’ll get that email out by tomorrow. And I’ll look into the transit thing first thing.
MARGARET (turning toward the door): Alright then. Goodnight, dear.
CASEY (catches themselves): Goodnight, Margaret. Text me when you get home safe — oh, right. Call me, I mean. If you’d like.

Margaret gives a small wave and walks out into the evening. Casey pulls out their phone and starts drafting the email.

Reflection Questions

  1. Intention: What does Casey want for Margaret? At what point does their helpfulness start to serve Casey’s needs more than Margaret’s?
  2. Tension: Margaret says, “I just wonder sometimes if the church has moved on from people like me.” What is she really asking — and does Casey answer it?
  3. Attention: When Margaret talks about the quiet house, forty years of Thursday dinners, and feeling invisible in breakout groups — what is she communicating? What does Casey hear instead?
  4. Retention: Casey will leave this conversation feeling they’ve helped. Margaret will leave this conversation feeling __________. Fill in the blank and explain.
  5. At what moment does the conversation shift from connection to problem-solving? What triggers that shift?
  6. Margaret has decades of experience in this congregation. How might Casey draw on that experience rather than positioning Margaret as someone who needs resources?

Facilitator Guidance

Running a 20-Minute Group Discussion

Minutes 1–5: Read Together

Assign three readers — one for Casey, one for Margaret, and one for the narration (stage directions in italics). Read the full scenario aloud. Ask the group to notice where the energy of the conversation changes — where does it shift from being with Margaret to being about Margaret?

Minutes 5–12: Individual Responses

Go around the table. Each person selects one question. Encourage specificity — quote lines, name the exact moment. The facilitator records themes. If the group is large, pair people briefly (2 minutes) before sharing out.

Minutes 12–20: Guided Synthesis

Draw out the pattern: Casey starts present (asking about Jean, listening to feelings) and gradually shifts to logistics and solutions. Ask the group: “When do you recognize yourself in Casey? When do you recognize yourself in Margaret?” Guide toward the insight that being heard is itself a form of care — and that an email full of links, however well-intentioned, can feel like being handed off. Close with: “What would ‘staying present’ have looked like for the last five minutes of this conversation?”

Sample Answers

Intention: Casey genuinely wants Margaret to keep coming and to feel supported. But as the conversation goes on, Casey shifts from listening to solving — and the accumulating list of resources starts to serve Casey’s need to feel effective more than Margaret’s need to feel seen. The email becomes a way to feel like the interaction was “handled.”

Tension: Margaret is asking whether she still belongs — whether the community values her presence, her history, her way of being in the world. Casey responds with logistics (“that’s not okay, let me help”) rather than sitting with the weight of the question. The deepest ouch may be the gentlest: Margaret says “it helps just knowing you care,” and Casey responds by offering to compile a resource list.

Attention: Margaret is communicating loneliness, grief, and displacement — the loss of Jean, the shrinking social circle, the feeling of being out of step with a community she helped build. Casey hears a transportation problem and a technology gap — solvable issues. The emotional core goes unaddressed.

Retention: Margaret will likely leave feeling cared about but not quite reached. She may feel the warmth of Casey’s effort but also the distance of being treated as a problem to solve. She may or may not open the email. What she wanted — to be with someone — happened in the first few minutes and then slipped away.

The shift begins at “Have you tried any of the ride-share apps?” — Casey moves from listening mode to fix-it mode. Each time Margaret redirects toward the emotional reality (“the ride is only part of it,” “it’s not just the technology”), Casey briefly acknowledges it and then returns to solutions. The email offer is the culmination — a generous gesture that also closes the conversation.

Casey could ask Margaret what she thinks would help — both for herself and for other longtime members who may feel similarly. Margaret’s deacon experience, her decades of relationships, and her institutional memory are assets. Instead of “let me send you resources,” Casey could say “would you be willing to help me think about how we make this space work better for everyone?”

More Ideal Choices

What Casey Did What Casey Could Do Instead
Suggested ride-share apps Asked: “What would make the trip feel manageable and worth it for you?”
Offered to research Access-a-Ride Said: “Let me walk with you on this — can I call you this week and we’ll figure it out together?”
Noted the digital/paper gap as a logistical fix “What would help you feel more connected in the breakout groups — not just the format, but the people?”
Compiled an email of resources and links Offered to sit with Margaret after the next meeting and introduce her to a few newer members personally
“I’ll get that email out by tomorrow” “Before I go looking things up — is there anything else you wanted to talk about tonight? I’m not in a rush.”

UU Principles in Play

Inherent worth and dignity: Margaret’s worth isn’t contingent on her ability to navigate new technology or adapt to a changing congregation. Her presence and history are contributions.

Justice and equity: Equity means examining who the group’s systems are designed for. If the default mode of participation assumes smartphones, laptops, and digital fluency, that’s a structural barrier — not a personal deficit.

Interdependence: Casey and Margaret need each other. The congregation needs Margaret’s institutional memory and relational depth as much as it needs Casey’s energy and initiative. Care flows in both directions.

Generosity: True generosity here isn’t the volume of resources offered — it’s the willingness to stay in an uncomfortable, unsolved moment with another person.

Accountability (8th Principle): Ageism and generational assumptions are forms of marginalization that UU communities must actively examine. Casey’s default to tech solutions and info-dumping, however kind, reflects a pattern of centering younger norms as universal.
