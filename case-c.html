Case Study C: “One-Size Prayer”

Theme: Cultural Competence

Format: 4-voice — spoken dialogue + inner monologue. Assign four readers or two readers doubling inner voices.

MARY — white Protestant chaplain, 68, lifelong church elder — warm, softly spoken

LINDA — Asian-American woman, 70, longtime Beacon member & practicing Buddhist, grieving the recent death of her childhood friend Mei

Scene

MARY (spoken, entering softly): Good afternoon, Linda. I’ve kept you close in prayer since Mei passed. How is your spirit today?
MARY (inner): Be present, acknowledge the loss.
LINDA (spoken, voice thin): The waves of sorrow still come. Breathing and chanting steady me.
LINDA (inner): I hope she remembers I’m Buddhist.
MARY (spoken, settling): Breathing sounds healing. May I first offer a brief blessing that comforts me in times of grief?
MARY (inner): Share without imposing.
LINDA (spoken): Yes — silence afterward would help.
LINDA (inner): Space to breathe after, good.
MARY (spoken, soft): “May peace rest upon you, and may Mei rejoice now in heaven’s gentle embrace, watched over by the Holy One.”
MARY (inner): A picture of hope beyond; surely universal?
LINDA (inner): Heaven? In my tradition we speak of rebirth, not heaven. Accept her kindness, but clarify.
MARY (spoken): Let’s share three breaths of silence for Mei.

Both breathe together slowly.

LINDA (spoken, gently): Mary, thank you. In Buddhism we imagine loved ones journeying onward toward awakening. Your blessing was kind, though the word heaven lands differently for me.
LINDA (inner): Offer teaching without scolding.
MARY (spoken, warm but reflective): I appreciate you telling me. I’m sorry — I slipped into my own language. Pluralism is a practice, isn’t it?
MARY (inner): Humility, learn.
LINDA (spoken): Exactly. I value that our traditions can sit side by side, even in grief. Thank you for the peace your words carried; may I share a loving-kindness verse for Mei and for us?
LINDA (inner): Reciprocity, invitation.
MARY (spoken, nodding): Please — I’d be honored. Next visit I can bring a meditation bell, if that feels supportive.
MARY (inner): Adapt to her practice.
LINDA (spoken, softly): That would be wonderful.
LINDA (inner): We’re learning together — this is pluralism alive.

Reflection Questions

  1. Intention: Mary asks permission before offering a blessing and genuinely means to comfort Linda. Where does her intention succeed, and where does it fall short despite her care?
  2. Tension: Mary says, “May Mei rejoice now in heaven’s gentle embrace.” Linda is Buddhist. What is the impact of that language — even though it’s offered with love?
  3. Attention: Notice how Linda’s inner monologue says “I hope she remembers I’m Buddhist” early on. What does it mean that Linda has to hope her chaplain remembers her faith tradition?
  4. Retention: This conversation ends with mutual warmth and a plan for next time. What will each person carry forward — and what made the repair possible?
  5. Unlike the other case studies, this scenario shows a misstep and a recovery. What skills does Mary demonstrate when she receives Linda’s feedback? What makes her response effective?
  6. Have you seen or experienced moments like this in your congregation? What makes it possible — or difficult — to give feedback about spiritual language to someone who is caring for you?

Facilitator Guidance

Running a 20-Minute Group Discussion

Minutes 1–5: Read Together

Assign four readers — one for Mary’s spoken lines, one for Mary’s inner monologue, one for Linda’s spoken lines, and one for Linda’s inner monologue. Read the full scenario aloud. Ask the group to notice the moment the conversation shifts — from care that assumes, to care that learns.

Minutes 5–12: Individual Responses

Go around the table. Each person selects one reflection question and shares their response. This scenario may invite more personal sharing about faith backgrounds — hold space for that while keeping focus on the dynamics of the interaction. The facilitator or a recorder captures key phrases.

Minutes 12–20: Guided Synthesis

This case study is unique because it models repair. Guide the group to name what makes the repair work: Linda gives feedback without blame (“the word heaven lands differently for me”), Mary receives it without defensiveness (“I slipped into my own language”), and both move toward reciprocity. Ask: “What conditions need to exist in a relationship for this kind of feedback to be possible?” Close with: “What’s one spiritual assumption you carry that you might need to examine?”

Sample Answers

Intention: Mary intends genuine comfort. She asks permission, offers what is meaningful to her, and creates space for silence. The misstep isn’t in her heart — it’s in defaulting to her own theological framework (“heaven,” “the Holy One”) as if it were universal. Her inner thought — “surely universal?” — reveals the assumption.

Tension: The word “heaven” lands as an erasure of Linda’s Buddhist worldview, even though Mary means it as comfort. For Linda, Mei is not “in heaven” — she is journeying toward awakening. The gap between these frameworks is not small. Linda’s grace in naming it doesn’t diminish the weight of being spiritually misrecognized while grieving.

Attention: The fact that Linda has to hope her chaplain remembers her tradition suggests this isn’t the first time Christian language has been the default. In a pluralist community, the burden of reminding others about your faith should not fall on the person in grief. Knowing a congregant’s tradition is part of the chaplain’s preparation, not the congregant’s responsibility.

Retention: Both will carry forward the warmth of the exchange and the plan for a meditation bell. Mary will remember the lesson about language. Linda will remember being heard — eventually. The repair was possible because of their existing relationship, Linda’s willingness to teach, and Mary’s capacity for humility without collapse.

Mary’s recovery skills are worth naming: she doesn’t get defensive, she doesn’t over-apologize, she doesn’t make it about her feelings. She names her mistake (“I slipped into my own language”), affirms the learning (“pluralism is a practice”), and takes a concrete step (offering to bring a meditation bell). This is a model of accountable response.

Giving feedback about spiritual language is hard because faith is intimate. Linda risks seeming ungrateful or picky in a moment of grief. What makes it possible here is the length of their relationship, Mary’s openness, and Linda’s framing (“teaching without scolding”). Not every congregant will feel safe doing this — which is why chaplains should learn traditions proactively, not reactively.

More Ideal Choices

What Mary Said What Mary Could Say Instead
“May Mei rejoice now in heaven’s gentle embrace, watched over by the Holy One.” “May peace surround you, and may Mei’s journey onward be gentle.” (tradition-neutral language, or ask Linda what words would feel right)
Offering a blessing from her own tradition first “I’d love to offer words of comfort. Would you prefer something from your tradition, or shall I share from mine, or both?”
Assuming “heaven” is a universal comfort image Preparing for the visit by reviewing Linda’s faith tradition and selecting language accordingly

UU Principles in Play

Pluralism: This scenario is pluralism in action — not as an ideal we profess, but as a practice we stumble through together. Mary’s willingness to learn and Linda’s willingness to teach are both acts of faith.

Free and responsible search for truth and meaning: Linda’s Buddhist practice is not a secondary or lesser framework. Honoring the free search means not defaulting to Christian theological language as the “normal” mode of spiritual care.

Inherent worth and dignity: Dignified care means meeting people in their tradition, not asking them to receive comfort through ours.

Accountability (8th Principle): Christian normativity is a form of cultural dominance, even in UU spaces. Accountability means doing the homework before the pastoral visit — not relying on the grieving person to correct us.
